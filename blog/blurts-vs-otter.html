Blurts vs Otter: personal task capture or a searchable conversation?

Compare Blurts and Otter for spoken tasks, meeting transcripts, action items, context, costs, and deciding whether to use one or both.

By the Blurts team November 9, 2026 5 min read
Decision Blurts Otter
Recording context Short personal intentions and reminders Meetings and conversations
Output Tasks for your list and supported destinations Transcripts, summaries and action items
Connection between them No verified Otter connector Meeting action extraction already exists

Start with what you need to keep from the conversation

Choose Otter when the conversation itself is valuable: you need to revisit what was said, find a decision, or share a record with someone who was not there. Consider Blurts when you mainly want to express your own intentions and turn them into clean tasks. Both involve speech, but the information you want afterward can be very different.

A meeting transcript and a personal task list should not be treated as interchangeable outputs. A transcript preserves context that a short task may omit. A task list can be easier to act on when the context is already clear. The right comparison starts with the future moment when you will use the result, rather than the fact that both tools accept audio.

Otter already identifies and manages action items

Otter’s product overview describes transcription, searchable meeting knowledge, and follow-up work. Its action-item documentation explains how users can see assigned items across conversations, edit them, and return to the relevant transcript context. Otter should not be described as merely producing a wall of text or as unable to identify next steps.

That context link can be valuable when an action is ambiguous. If a short task says to revise a proposal, the discussion may explain which section, whose feedback, and what tradeoff the team chose. A workflow that keeps the evidence close to the task can reduce the need to ask everyone to reconstruct the meeting later.

What Blurts is being evaluated for

Blurts focuses on turning a spoken ramble into clean tasks. The relevant input may be a short set of personal intentions rather than an entire meeting. For example, after reading a document you may already know what you want to do and simply need to preserve those actions before moving on.

Blurts is built around recordings of up to two minutes and the tasks extracted from them. It saves the resulting tasks, lets you edit their details, and can send them to Notion, Google Tasks, or Microsoft Planner. Its voice capture guide explains the review and delivery steps. That is a different scope from retaining a long meeting with speaker context and a searchable transcript. There is no direct Otter connector documented here. Use Blurts for a short personal follow-up when that is the missing step; retain a meeting tool when the conversation itself must remain available.

Compare a meeting with a personal debrief

For a meeting use case, ask whether you need the participants’ actual words, the reasoning behind a decision, and a shared record. If so, evaluate Otter’s conversation workflow directly. A personal recap recorded afterward reflects what you remember and choose to include. It cannot establish a complete record of everything other people said.

For a personal-capture use case, imagine you have just finished your own planning session. You want to renew a membership, request a quote, and check a date before committing to an event. The desired result is a small set of actions, with the uncertainty around the event preserved. Compare how much review each approach requires before those tasks are somewhere you will act on them.

  • Separate tasks you personally intend to do from actions assigned to other people.
  • Preserve conditions such as “after approval” instead of treating them as completed decisions.
  • Check whether you need the original conversation context or only the resulting task.

Review effort matters more than transcript length

A long transcript is not inherently a failure, and a short task list is not inherently a success. The transcript can answer questions a summary leaves open. The task list can also include an instruction that nobody actually agreed to. Review the specific output you depend on: wording, ownership, timing, and conditions.

For your own spoken thoughts, check that an idea remained an idea when appropriate. “Maybe ask for a quote” and “request the quote today” do not express the same commitment. For meetings, check whether a suggested action was accepted and who agreed to own it. These checks apply to AI-assisted outputs generally; this article does not claim one product has superior recognition accuracy.

Compare costs against the workload you really have

Otter’s plan comparison distinguishes free and paid offerings and describes transcription, imports, usage allowances, and collaboration features. Read the current terms for your recording method rather than assuming every audio workflow has the same limit. A user recording regular meetings has a different workload from someone capturing a few personal tasks between activities.

For Blurts, compare the current offer with your own capture frequency and the amount of cleanup required. Avoid paying for a full conversation workflow solely because it can receive audio if all you need is a task list. Equally, avoid replacing a useful meeting archive with short tasks if you routinely need to retrieve decisions and their context.

Using both can be reasonable with a clear boundary

Keep Otter for conversations whose record you need. Test Blurts for new personal thoughts or follow-ups that arise outside those recordings. If both tools produce an action for the same commitment, choose a single place where its completion is tracked. Otherwise, you may gain two copies of a task and uncertainty about which one is current.

Do not assume a direct Blurts-to-Otter connection or an automatic transfer into your preferred task manager. Verify any supported integration separately. The useful boundary is based on the outcome: preserve a conversation when you need evidence and context; capture a personal task when you already know the action and need to remember it.

Common questions

Does Otter already extract action items?

Yes. Otter documents action-item management and a way to inspect the related transcript context. It is a substantive alternative for meeting-derived work.

Can a personal Blurts recap replace a meeting transcript?

A recap records your selected recollection and intentions. It should not be treated as a complete record of another person’s statements or all the decisions made in a meeting.

When does using both make sense?

When you need both searchable meeting records and a separate way to capture personal thoughts. Keep one authoritative task-review routine and avoid duplicate tracking of the same commitment.
